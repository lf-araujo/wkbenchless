#+TITLE: The Citation DAG: A Serverless Ledger for Scientific Publishing
#+AUTHOR: Luis F. Araujo
#+STARTUP: nohideblocks

Draft outline for the paper. The working system is in src/wkbdag.nim
(ledger), extensions/dag_ledger.nim (editor verbs), extensions/tox_collab.nim
(review channel), src/wkbbib.nim (BibTeX bridge).

* Abstract (one paragraph)

A citation graph is a directed acyclic graph, so the ledger's topology
can mirror the content's topology: papers are nodes, citations are
edges, and the registration fee gives the DAG an economic layer. We
present a working system -- written in Nim, integrated into a literate
editor -- where papers are committed to a DAG ledger after a
three-reviewer threshold, citations flow from the .bib file (the
citation graph extracts itself), and coins reward both cited authors
and reviewers. No editorial board, no platform, no server.

* 1. Introduction: the editorial bottleneck

- The current system: submit to a journal, wait for an editor to find
  reviewers, wait for reviews, wait for a decision. Months to years.
- The cost is not the review -- it's the coordination overhead.
- Prior alternatives (DAP, Tenorio, Mackey, ScienceMatters, eLife, PCI)
  all replace the editor with a DAO, a platform, or a central
  authority. None remove the intermediary.
- Our contribution: the citation graph IS the ledger. No intermediary
  because the infrastructure is the content.

* 2. The DAG-citation isomorphism

- A citation graph is a DAG by construction (papers cite earlier papers).
- The ledger's topology mirrors the content's topology:
  - Nodes = papers (identified by content hash)
  - Edges = citations (verified against committed papers)
  - Consensus = 3-reviewer threshold (not PoW/PoS)
  - Economic layer = registration fees flow to cited authors
- Acyclicity is guaranteed by construction: citations must point to
  already-committed papers. No cycle detection needed.
- Retractions are append-only: a new node linked to the retracted one.
  Nothing is mutated or removed.

* 3. The BibTeX bridge: the citation graph extracts itself

- The key insight: the .bib file IS the citation graph.
- Each committed paper gets a `dagid` field in its BibTeX entry.
- When another author cites it, the DAG parses their .bib and builds
  the edge automatically. No manual citation registration.
- The genesis registry maps DOIs to DAG IDs for papers imported from
  the traditional system. Dangling edges (non-DAG citations) are skipped.
- Authors write papers in LaTeX/org as usual. The DAG is invisible
  infrastructure.

* 4. The economic model

- Registration fee: the author pays F coins to submit.
- Fee split: 70% to cited authors (split evenly), 30% to reviewers
  (split evenly among accepters). This incentivizes both citation
  and review participation.
- Faucet: new identities receive a small amount (5 coins) once, so
  they can pay their first registration fee. Sybil-resistant because
  the amount is too small to be profitable.
- Anti-gaming:
  - Reviewers must have stake (a committed paper or a coin balance).
  - Reviewers cannot review their own paper.
  - The faucet is once-only per identity.
- Coin supply: fixed at genesis. No inflation. Coins circulate from
  authors to cited authors to reviewers. The economy is deflationary
  (coins concentrate with highly-cited authors), which is a feature:
  it rewards sustained contribution.

* 5. The review channel

- Reviews travel over Tox (a serverless, encrypted P2P network).
- The editor (wkbenchless) wraps Tox as a contact list + inbox --
  structurally identical to email, but without the SMTP layer.
- The author submits from their editor; the paper travels to
  reviewers' editors; they accept or reject; the review travels back.
- Reviewer discovery: initially manual (the author contacts reviewers
  via Tox). As the DAG grows, the citation graph itself provides
  recommendations (co-citation, bibliographic coupling).

* 6. Implementation

- Written in Nim: compiled, low-level, C-interop, no runtime.
  The first DAG blockchain in Nim (a notable gap in the ecosystem).
- ~500 lines of core ledger code (wkbdag.nim), ~120 lines of BibTeX
  parser (wkbbib.nim), ~200 lines of editor integration.
- 49 unit tests covering the full lifecycle, coin flow, anti-gaming.
- Integrated into wkbenchless, a literate Nim editor with org-babel,
  REPL sessions, and P2P collaborative editing (also built on Tox).

* 7. Related work

| System | Approach | Key difference |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| DAP | Blockchain + smart contracts | Centralised virtual editor, Ethereum |
| Tenorio et al. (2021) | Blockchain + IPFS | IPFS storage, no DAG, no coin economy |
| Mackey et al. | DAO governance | Voting, not 3-reviewer threshold |
| ScienceMatters | Centralised platform | Not P2P, no DAG |
| eLife Reviewed Preprints | Single organisation | No blockchain, no coin economy |
| Peer Community In | Human governance | No blockchain, no Tox |
| Philica (2001) | Author finds 3 reviewers | No blockchain, no coin economy |

Our novelty: (1) DAG-as-citation-graph (semantic ledger structure),
(2) coin flow on citation edges, (3) Tox as the review channel,
(4) no editorial board, no DAO, no platform.

* 8. Discussion: hard problems

- Reviewer discovery: bootstraps manually, transitions to DAG-based
  recommendation as the graph grows.
- Rubber-stamping: mitigated by stake requirements and the economic
  cost of reviewing (time spent, not coins).
- Adoption: the discoverability system (separate work) is the critical
  dependency. Without indexing, the citation economy never starts.
- Key loss: social recovery (k-of-n trusted contacts) or hardware
  keys, same as crypto wallets.

* 9. Conclusion

The citation DAG replaces the editorial system with a data structure.
The ledger is the content; the consensus is the review; the economy
is the citation. No server, no platform, no intermediary -- just
scientists, their papers, and a graph.
